# Candidate Invariant Tests

## Current candidate invariant

> As machine intelligence becomes cheaper, the scarce capability may become establishing a defensible state of reality for consequential decisions.

This is a candidate invariant, not an established law, validated market, or permission to build a product.

## Three capabilities that must not be conflated

### 1. Information extraction

Find relevant signals in a large volume of data.

Example: identify observations that may indicate an unusual movement or event.

AI is becoming increasingly capable here. Excellence at extraction alone is unlikely to prove the thesis.

### 2. State estimation

Infer what is probably true about the world from available signals.

Example: estimate the sustainable cash generation and current obligations of a business.

This adds interpretation and uncertainty, but a probable state estimate is not automatically sufficient to justify action.

### 3. Decision-grade verification

Establish that a state claim is sufficiently trustworthy and defensible for a specified consequential action.

Example: defend why an estimated business state supports a particular working-capital facility under an accepted error threshold.

This requires provenance, visible contradictions, explicit confidence, challengeability, reproducibility, and an accountable link to the decision standard.

## Where the candidate invariant may live

The primitive is not merely capability 1. The open thesis is whether capability 2—and especially capability 3—remains scarce even as models become far better and cheaper.

## Conditions required for infrastructure

Decision-Grade Truth becomes an unavoidable infrastructure layer only if enough of the following remain true:

1. Consequential decisions become increasingly machine-mediated.
2. Uncertainty about source, state, provenance, or outcome remains costly.
3. Better models cannot cheaply infer a sufficient state from existing data alone.
4. Evidence external to the model remains necessary.
5. Actors will pay or commit resources to reduce the uncertainty.
6. Existing institutions do not already solve the problem well enough.
7. The process can be standardized and repeated without relying on one expert.
8. The problem recurs across unrelated domains for structural—not superficial—reasons.
9. The output can be connected to a real decision standard and accountable action.

## Counterexamples to seek

Actively search for domains or workflows where:

- AI already makes reliable decisions without external verification;
- strong verification was never economically necessary;
- physical capacity, access, regulation, or authority is the true bottleneck;
- truth is available but decision-makers do not pay for it;
- incumbents already provide sufficient verification cheaply;
- better models make missing context irrelevant;
- a useful state estimate cannot be made sufficiently defensible;
- each domain requires bespoke judgment, preventing repeatable infrastructure;
- verification cost exceeds the expected cost of decision error.

## Falsification questions

- If models become 100 times better, does the problem disappear?
- If inference becomes nearly free, what evidence still has to come from reality?
- What decision cannot proceed safely or economically without defensibility?
- Who bears the cost of uncertainty, and do they pay to reduce it?
- What does an incumbent already know or control?
- What would reality look like if this thesis were wrong?

## Evidence rule

Recurrence in military intelligence, SME lending, health, robotics, logistics, or agent authorization is suggestive, not proof. A domain counts as supporting evidence only after a concrete workflow shows:

1. a consequential decision;
2. an unresolved state claim;
3. a costly evidence or defensibility gap;
4. failure of current alternatives;
5. measurable value from closing the gap.
