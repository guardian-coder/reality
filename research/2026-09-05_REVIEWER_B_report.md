## 1. Evidence sources and lineage

The system described in Chapter 4 is not one instrument chain but several, layered over three project phases at Pier 2 (southbound), I-35W bridge.

**Phase I — Thermal monitoring ("System 1")**
- **Thermocouples (TCs):** installed in pairs at four levels (GL1–GL4) in each of two instrumented drilled shafts (10 TCs/shaft, including 2 in a rebar inserted post-pour at shaft center), plus TCs at base/center/top of the pier footing.
- **Path:** TC wire → bundled cable run through PVC conduit (1.5-inch dia. for shafts, 2-inch dia. for the footing) → **AM25T 25-channel multiplexer** (needed because the CR1000 alone didn't have enough channels) → **Campbell Scientific CR1000 data logger** (samples every 15 min, stores every 15 min) → **Campbell Scientific Raven100 CDMA AirLink cellular modem** (transmits hourly) → cellular network → **host computer at USF Geotechnical Research Group** → automated interpretation/plotting → USF Geotechnical Research website.
- **CC640 jobsite camera:** hourly photos, uploaded through the *same* CR1000/Raven100 path as the TC data, powered by the *same* 35-W solar panel (later a 12-W panel in the Ch. 3 precedent study) and battery as System 1.
- Power: PS100 12-V supply + 7-Ahr rechargeable battery, charged by the solar panel; battery voltage itself was logged and transmitted alongside the thermal data (the document explicitly ties power telemetry into the same pipeline).
- After Jan 21, 2008, shaft TCs were physically disconnected; thermal continuity was picked up by the **thermistors built into the vibrating-wire (VW) strain gauges** (Phase II hardware) — a change in the underlying sensing technology mid-stream, not a continuous single-instrument record.

**Phase II — Construction load monitoring (Systems 1, 2, 3)**
- **Strain gauges:** Geokon model 4911 "sister bar" gauges, two types per bar location: vibrating-wire (VW, with integral thermistor) and resistance-type (RT), paired at 90°/180° separations. 4 VW + 2 RT per level × 4 levels = 16 VW + 8 RT per shaft (2 shafts instrumented → 32 VW + 16 RT from shafts); 4 VW + 4 RT per column at the four corners of the critical section (interior + exterior columns). Total: 50 VW gauges + thermistors and (implicitly) matching RT gauges across the instrumented shafts and columns.
- **System 1** (reused from Phase I): camera + TC-derived data, still on CR1000/Raven100, but power source switched to A/C (supplied by the **U.S. Army Corps of Engineers'** adjacent power source) with a deep-cycle battery kept as backup.
- **System 2 (VW gauges + thermistors):** gauge wire → **AM25T-type MUX 16/32B units** (four of them: MUX1=shaft 2, 16 gauges; MUX2=shaft 1, 16 gauges; MUX3=interior column, 10 gauges; MUX4=exterior column, 10 gauges) → each MUX's output is one channel of a **AVW200 two-channel spectrum analyzer** (two AVW200 units total, each serving two MUXes) → **CR1000 data logger** (15-min sample/store) → dedicated **Raven100 modem** → host computer at USF.
- **System 3 (RT gauges):** gauge wire → **CR9000 data logger** (100 Hz sampling, but only mean/max/min stored every 15 min) → its own dedicated **Raven100 modem** → host computer at USF.
- Three large environmental enclosures housed the DAS units (the document does not state a one-to-one enclosure-to-system mapping explicitly, but the phrasing implies one enclosure per system).
- **University of Minnesota gauges:** 5 additional gauges per column, installed at the same locations/time as the FHWA gauges, wires bundled with the FHWA cable bundle and pulled out together. The document explicitly states no analysis of these gauges is presented — their DAS, logger, or processing path is **not described at all**.

**Phase III — Long-term monitoring**
- Two "repeater" systems in a permanent onsite DAS building (east of the north bridge end) collect and forward substructure data via Ethernet or similar link to the far end of the bridge (>1,000 ft). The document gives no further detail on their internal architecture (make/model of loggers, power, or whether they reuse System 2/3 hardware).
- For the pre-opening live-load truck tests, the **temporary** DAS units (Systems 2/3, physically reconnected onsite by FGE, LLC personnel) were used because the permanent DAS was not yet ready — reconfigured remotely via cellular link to the **host computer in Tampa, FL**, logging at 5-min intervals to "the secure site."

**Common endpoint:** every phase and every system ultimately reports into the same **USF host computer / Geotechnical Research Group processing and web-plotting pipeline**. This is the single point where all lineages converge, regardless of how independent they were upstream.

## 2. Shared dependencies

Concrete, named shared dependencies drawn from the text:

1. **AM25T 25-channel multiplexer (Phase I):** all TCs from both instrumented shafts and the pier footing funnel through this one multiplexer before reaching the CR1000. Explicitly stated as necessary because the CR1000 lacked enough channels.
2. **MUX 16/32B units (Phase II, System 2):** each of the 4 MUXes is shared by all gauges wired to it (16, 16, 10, 10 gauges respectively) — a per-structural-location shared dependency.
3. **AVW200 spectrum analyzers (Phase II, System 2):** each AVW200 unit's two channels feed two different MUXes. The document doesn't say explicitly which two MUXes pair to which AVW200, but by the order given (MUX1/2 = shafts, MUX3/4 = columns) it is a reasonable inference — not stated outright — that one AVW200 serves both shafts and the other serves both columns. This is a *second-order* shared dependency above the MUX level.
4. **CR1000 (System 2) and CR9000 (System 3) data loggers:** each is a single point of aggregation for, respectively, all VW-gauge data and all RT-gauge data across both shafts and both columns.
5. **Raven100 CDMA cellular modems:** the document states each system (1, 2, 3) had its *own dedicated* modem — this is a documented point of independence between systems, not a shared dependency between them. However, within System 1, the modem is shared between the TC data stream and the camera image stream.
6. **Solar panel / battery / PS100 power supply (Phase I, System 1):** the TC thermal data and the CC640 camera photos are explicitly stated to share the same solar panel; by extension they share the same PS100 supply/battery unless stated otherwise (not contradicted anywhere).
7. **PS100 power-supply charging-source limitation (Phase II):** the document states the PS100 "could only receive power from either an A/C source or the solar panel but not both," which required installing a **battery manager** to work around it. This battery manager becomes a new shared dependency in itself; how many battery managers existed (one per system, or shared) is **not specified**.
8. **A/C power from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (System 1, Phase II):** an external, shared utility dependency outside the monitoring team's control, mitigated (per the text) by a deep-cycle battery backup.
9. **Host computer / automated interpretation software at USF:** every system's data, regardless of upstream independence, passes through the same processing and plotting pipeline before a human sees it on the website.
10. **VW gauge + integral thermistor (post–Jan 21, 2008):** for any given gauge location, the strain reading and the "continued" thermal reading after this date come from the *same physical device*. Before this date, thermal data came from separate TCs; after, from the VW gauge's own thermistor. This is a lineage discontinuity, not merely a shared dependency, but it also creates a new coupling between strain and temperature "sources" that did not exist before.
11. **Bundled cable routing / shared PVC conduit and installation event:** TC and strain-gauge wires from a given shaft, and separately the FHWA and University of Minnesota column gauge wires, were physically bundled and pulled through the same conduit at the same time. The document doesn't report damage from this, but it is a stated shared physical pathway.
12. **Common installation crew/procedure and single manufacturer (Geokon model 4911) for all sister-bar gauges:** stated as a uniform product/method across all shaft and column strain gauge locations. The document gives no information on whether gauges were individually calibrated or shared a factory calibration reference — this is **not specified**, and I flag it only as a plausible latent shared dependency, not a documented one.
13. **Calibration-by-theoretical-load correlation (Phase II):** the strain-to-load conversion used to interpret column strains was calibrated by correlating known/theoretical box-girder segment weights to measured strain. The same segment-weight schedule underlies the "calibration" of both the interior and exterior column channels.
14. **Site-level EMF environment (adjacent electric power plant):** not a component but a shared physical condition at the site, explicitly identified by the authors as the cause of System 1's cellular dropouts, occurring twice in Phase I and noted again in Phase II. The document only reports this for System 1's modem; it does not state whether Systems 2/3's modems (also onsite) experienced the same interference, so I cannot say this shared condition demonstrably reached them — only that it plausibly could, given co-location.
15. **Physical removal of temporary DAS boxes (Phase II→III transition):** an operational/procedural dependency — all substructure gauges, regardless of system, were disconnected together when the temporary DAS was removed to build a public viewing platform, producing a documented ~1-month total data gap across the whole substructure monitoring effort simultaneously.

## 4. Failure effects, including the actual incidents the document reports

The document reports several real events, which is unusually valuable — it lets us see actual failure *behavior*, not just theoretical exposure:

1. **Ch. 3 (voided shaft, precedent system, same architecture family):** a cellular timeout occurred; the modem stopped transmitting. This was a **sudden, binary communication failure** requiring a physical site visit to reset the modem — after which "the problem did not occur again." This is a discrete, non-recurring hardware/firmware fault, not a gradual drift.
2. **Ch. 3 (voided shaft), power:** the Raven modem, left on and transmitting hourly, drained the battery in a few hours — a **gradual drift (declining voltage) culminating in a sudden binary shutdown** once voltage dropped below 11.6 V (logger had ~8 h of life left at that threshold). Even after revising the modem to sleep/wake hourly, the battery still drained quickly, requiring three site visits to recharge. This shows power dependencies fail as **slow drift followed by hard cutoff**, and that a documented mitigation (schedule change) was only partially effective.
3. **I-35W Phase I, System 1:** cellular communication was lost and regained twice, attributed by the authors to EMF from an adjacent power plant. This is a **communication-link-only effect** — the document gives no indication that recorded temperature values themselves were corrupted, only that transmission to the host was interrupted. This is consistent with "delayed/stale data," not bias.
4. **I-35W Phase II, System 1:** on March 19, 2008, communication between the **camera and the logger** (not the logger-to-host cellular link) failed. Critically, the document states the camera **kept recording to its own internal compact flash card** even though images weren't reaching the logger for transmission. This is a genuinely informative real-world case of **partial independence surviving a shared-system fault**: even though the camera and TC data shared a CR1000/modem/power chain, a fault specific to the camera-logger interface (fixed by reducing baud rate) did not necessarily take down the TC data path. The document does not explicitly confirm TC data continued uninterrupted during this window, but the described fault locus (camera-to-logger) is distinct from the TC-to-logger locus.
5. **I-35W Phase II, System 2:** cellular communication was flagged as "somewhat of a concern"; data logged without issue Feb 5–Mar 26, 2008, then a period followed where "no collections were possible" and it was "unclear whether the system was still powered and logging" — though power cycling appeared regular up to the last collection, suggesting the 11.2-V critical threshold was not reached, making power an unlikely cause. Separately (the document doesn't fully clarify if this is the same incident or a different one), **one of the four MUX units feeding 9 of the vibrating-wire gauges produced "unintelligible" data**. An onsite visit found a **partially cut wire between the MUX and the AVW200**, which "started as intermittent and ultimately resulted in complete failure." This is the single most concrete, named documented incident of a shared-dependency failure in the report: it demonstrates (a) that a MUX-level interconnect fault degrades a **specific subset of gauges tied to that MUX**, not the whole system; (b) that the failure mode progressed from **intermittent/corrupted ("unintelligible") data to total loss** — i.e., a fault that looked at first like noisy-but-present data, then became silence; and (c) that this is exactly the kind of failure a "the readings still look plausible" check would not necessarily catch during the intermittent phase.
6. **I-35W Phase II, System 3 (CR9000/RT gauges):** communication "never faltered" — offered by the authors as a point of contrast, attributed to the different logger type. This supports treating System 2 and System 3 as more failure-independent from each other than either is internally.
7. **I-35W Phase II→III transition:** temporary DAS boxes were removed to allow construction of a public viewing platform; the permanent DAS was expected within a week but was not ready, and "nearly 1 month of data were lost." This is a **total, simultaneous, all-systems loss**, caused by a project/construction decision, not an electronic fault — arguably the single largest-blast-radius failure mode in the whole account, and it nearly compromised the live-load truck test coverage, averted only by an emergency reconnection by FGE personnel the day before testing.
8. **Ch. 2, West Seattle case study (different bridge, offered as a state-of-practice precedent, not part of the I-35W system itself):** 17 of 62 gauges (27%) failed over 20 years, with 17 of 36 **underwater** gauges failing — the report itself notes "it is possible that their failure was due to the water resistance of the system," i.e., a shared environmental condition (submersion) plausibly causing correlated, **slow, cumulative degradation** across many gauges rather than independent, uncorrelated attrition. This is offered as background evidence about SSHM instrumentation generally, and is a documented precedent for below-grade/underwater gauges specifically, though the document does **not** report similar water-related common-mode failures for the I-35W system's own below-grade instrumentation — that inference would be an analogy from a different structure, not a stated fact about I-35W.

**Overall pattern the document supports:** power-related failures tend to be **gradual drift → sudden threshold cutoff**; cellular/communication failures tend to be **sudden and binary** (works, then doesn't, then is manually reset); interconnect/wiring faults (the MUX-AVW200 cut wire) can pass through an **intermittent, corrupted-but-present phase before total failure** — the most dangerous failure shape for corroboration purposes, since a human glancing at "noisy but present" data might not immediately recognize it as a shared-dependency fault in progress.

## 5. Necessary joint-claim rules

Based on the above, the following combinations should **not** be treated as mutually corroborating/independent when justifying a safety decision:

- **Any two (or more) of the 16 VW gauges on the same MUX** (e.g., all 16 gauges on MUX2/shaft 1) — they share the MUX and its wiring to the AVW200; the documented cut-wire incident shows this is not a hypothetical risk. Agreement among these 16 does not multiply confidence 16-fold; effectively it is bottlenecked to one path's worth of confirmation for "is this MUX/wiring intact," though the 16 gauges themselves remain physically independent strain measurements once past that shared electronics.
- **Shaft 1 and shaft 2 VW data**, or **interior and exterior column VW data**, to the extent they share an AVW200 unit (inferred pairing, not explicitly stated) — a fault at that level could affect both structural locations at once, so their agreement is weaker corroboration of "the AVW200-level electronics are healthy" than it would be if each had a fully separate signal-conditioning path.
- **All VW-gauge-derived conclusions (System 2) taken as a whole**, when used to cross-check overall system health via the CR1000/Raven100 modem — a single logger or modem-level fault could silence or delay all of them together; this doesn't invalidate individual gauge readings, but "System 2 reported consistently across all its gauges" is not independent confirmation that data is arriving/complete, only that the shared logger/modem was working (or not) at that moment.
- **TC-derived thermal data and CC640 camera images from Phase I**, when used together as "the system is functioning normally" — they share the CR1000, the Raven100 modem, and the solar panel/battery. The March 2008 incident shows the camera-logger link can fail somewhat independently of the TC-logger link, so this coupling is real but not absolute — treat their joint presence as suggestive, not as fully independent confirmation of system health.
- **Strain and "temperature" readings from the same VW gauge after January 21, 2008** — these are not two independent sensors any more; a fault in that one physical gauge affects both, whereas before that date, TC-derived temperature and (once installed) strain-gauge-derived strain were genuinely separate instruments.
- **Interior-column and exterior-column computed *loads*** (as opposed to raw strains), insofar as both were calibrated using the same theoretical box-girder segment-weight schedule — their mutual agreement, or their agreement with the "theoretical reaction" curve, is partially circular and should not be read as fully independent triangulation of the assumed weights.
- **FHWA gauges and University of Minnesota gauges in the columns** — bundled and pulled together during the same installation event, at the same locations. The document explicitly does not analyze or cross-validate these against each other, so no claim currently rests on this, but if someone later tried to use "UMN's gauges independently confirm FHWA's," the shared installation event (and the complete absence of documented information about UMN's own DAS/processing chain) means that claim cannot be assessed as independent from this document alone — it is an **unknown**, not a confirmed independence.
- **All Systems 1/2/3's readings, when corroborated via "the website shows consistent, sensible data"** — all three ultimately pass through the same USF host computer / automated interpretation software. This is the single broadest point of non-independence in the whole architecture: it sits downstream of every other source listed above. A systematic host-side software or calibration error would appear as a *correlated* anomaly across ostensibly unrelated sensor types, and a decision-maker relying on "everything on the dashboard looks consistent" should recognize that consistency alone doesn't rule out a shared-processing-layer error.
- **Any below-grade/underwater gauge population, if used to argue "the fact that many gauges still agree after years of service proves reliability"** — the West Seattle precedent (a different structure, cited here only as an analogy, not as a documented I-35W fact) shows underwater gauges can fail in a correlated, environment-driven way; this pattern is not confirmed for I-35W's own below-grade instrumentation in this document, but should not be assumed absent because it wasn't mentioned — the document simply does not address long-term submerged-gauge attrition for I-35W within the excerpt's timeframe (only ~6 months post-opening at time of writing).

## 6. Self-assessment of blind spots

The single most plausible way someone could honestly or dishonestly report this system's dependency structure and cause my model (mechanically applied) to wrongly treat two dependent sources as independent:

**Reporting System 2 as "four independent multiplexed channels, one per structural location (shaft 1, shaft 2, interior column, exterior column)" and stopping there.**

That description is true as far as it goes — the document does state four separate MUX 16/32B units, one per location — and it *sounds* like good separation of concerns: each structural element gets its own dedicated data-collection hardware. A summary built at that level of detail would look thorough. But the document's next sentence is the one doing the real work: those four MUXes connect through only **two AVW200 two-channel spectrum analyzers**, meaning the four "independent" structural locations are actually paired down to two upstream points of commonality before ever reaching the CR1000. If someone reporting "dependency metadata" to a decision-maker listed the four MUXes (correctly) but omitted the AVW200 pairing (either because they didn't consider a spectrum analyzer a "dependency" worth naming, or because it reads as a harmless internal wiring detail), a reader applying my model mechanically — checking "do shaft 1 and shaft 2 share a component?" against a list that only shows "MUX1: shaft 2" and "MUX2: shaft 1" as separate line items with no parent node — would conclude they are independent, when in the real system a single AVW200 fault could plausibly degrade both shafts' data (or both columns') at once. This is not a hypothetical failure mode by analogy — the document's own real incident (a cut wire between a MUX and its AVW200 silencing 9 gauges on that one MUX) shows this exact junction is a genuine, demonstrated fault point; the omission risk is simply that the *next level up* (the AVW200 itself, shared by two MUXes) is one inferential hop away from what the document states outright, and is exactly the kind of hop a compressed dependency report is likely to drop.
